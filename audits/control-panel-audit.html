Control Panel Compliance Audit

Tenna ELD web control panel, screens W1 to W18 · 29 August 2026 · prepared by AppAxis

What this is. The companion to the mobile app audit. It checks the documented control panel against the duties the rules put on the motor carrier rather than on the driver.

What was compared. All 18 control panel screens at the 9/2 revision, their data elements and their drawn content, against 49 CFR Part 395 and Appendix A to Subpart B, with Section 3 of the proposal checked alongside.

The short version. The control panel is in better shape than the driver app. Every screen the rules ask a carrier to have is drawn, and the four duties that are easiest to fail are covered properly. What is missing is mostly around the edges of the ELD rule: obligations that sit in Part 395 next to the ELD sections, and one storage requirement that is an architecture decision rather than a screen.

Findings

1. Supporting documents have no home High

§395.11

Separately from the electronic logs, a carrier has to collect and keep up to eight supporting documents for each driver for each day worked: things like bills of lading, fuel receipts, dispatch records and payroll. Drivers have to hand them in within thirteen days. Auditors ask for them in the same breath as the logs, and they are used to check the logs are honest.

The words "supporting document" do not appear anywhere in the package. No screen collects them, none stores them, and the DOT audit export (W13) does not include them even though it claims to bundle "everything in the compliance record."

This may be a deliberate scope decision, since supporting documents are a Part 395 duty rather than an ELD function. If so it should be written down as one. As it stands, W13 promises an auditor something it cannot deliver.

2. No second copy of the records on separate storage High verify

§395.22, back-up copy requirement

A carrier has to keep a back-up copy of its ELD records on a device separate from the one holding the originals, and keep it for six months alongside them.

Nothing in the package acknowledges this. Section 3's backend requirements cover "6-month log retention with audit-ready archive," which is the retention but not the separate copy. The ownership split does not mention it either.

This is an architecture and operations requirement rather than a screen, which is probably why it was missed. It still has to be designed, and it affects where the data lives, which is exactly what the ownership split exists to answer.

3. Nothing tracks that drivers were given the materials the carrier owes them Medium

§395.22

A carrier has to supply every driver with four things: the ELD user guide, a malfunction instruction sheet, a data transfer instruction sheet, and enough blank paper log sheets to cover eight days.

The driver app delivers the first three, on D25, which is good. The fourth is physical paper and cannot be. No control panel screen records that a driver received any of it. In an audit the carrier is asked to show it happened.

Note that the mobile audit found the data transfer instruction sheet on D25 is currently wrong, because it still lists USB and Bluetooth. That is finding 3 in the companion audit and it matters here too, since supplying it is the carrier's duty.

4. Nothing detects one driver account in use on two devices Medium

§395.22, drivers must use their own accounts

The carrier is responsible for making sure each driver logs in as themselves. The package handles the version of this problem it already knew about: the shared "exempt driver" account is called out in D6, D27 and W15, and D27 exists specifically to remove the reason people create one.

What no screen does is notice the pattern. If one driver's credentials appear on two devices at once, or on a truck they are not assigned to, nothing surfaces it. Q9 asks a related hardware question, about two phones draining one tracker, but not this one.

Q24, raised in this revision, makes it sharper: on a shared cab tablet one enrolled face opens any driver's saved session. That is an account sharing risk created by a feature, and the control panel has no way to see it.

5. No path for a malfunction that cannot be fixed in eight days Medium verify

§395.34

W10 tracks the eight day clock properly and is one of the better screens in the module. What it does not cover is what happens when the clock runs out. A carrier that needs longer has to ask FMCSA for an extension in writing. Nothing in the package mentions that path, so the screen counts down to a deadline it has no answer for.

6. The inspector view inherits the mobile app's header gaps Low

W18 is described as using "the same renderer as D16," which is the right decision and saves work. It means the missing header fields found in the mobile audit are missing here too: trailer number, shipping document number, exempt driver status, the start of the 24 hour period and time zone, miles and hours today, and the malfunction indicator.

No separate work. Fixing D16 fixes this, which is the point of sharing the renderer. It is listed so the count is honest.

7. Nothing addresses using the ELD to harass drivers Low verify

The ELD rule includes protections against a carrier using the device to harass a driver, together with a complaint route for drivers. The word does not appear in the package.

The screens where this normally comes up are the ones that exist here: W1 shows live driver locations, and W5 lets a manager propose changes to somebody's log. The package already handles the adjacent question well, since P3 holds how long raw position data is kept as an open decision rather than assuming an answer. Worth a sentence saying the protection exists and where it lands.

8. Carrier settings could weaken compliance and nothing stops them Low

W15 is described as "the settings that shape the compliance engine without ever weakening it," which is the right intention. No screen shows how that is enforced. If a carrier can set a warning threshold, someone should establish which settings have floors and which do not. This is a build concern more than a design gap, and it is cheap to settle now.

What is already right

The four carrier duties that most often fail an audit are all covered, and covered properly.

  • Log edits. W5 proposes and never changes. The driver decides on D21 and the result comes back to W6. A carrier cannot alter a driver's log on its own, and the design makes that structural rather than a policy.
  • Unidentified driving. W16 is thorough: the full six month set, what the driver said, the two things a carrier is allowed to do, the author and timestamp on every annotation, the thirty minute daily threshold, the retention clock, and a nudge when the queue has not been worked. The assignment now returns to the driver on D34 for them to accept, which closes the loop.
  • Malfunctions. W10 tracks the eight day clock per device, with the driver's paper log acknowledgment beside it.
  • Retention and audit. Six months on W3, a bulk package on W13, per driver export on W7 with a record of who exported what, and eRODS transfers from the back office on W17 capped at thirty days per transfer.

W18 deserves a specific mention. Building a screen that shows a carrier exactly what an inspector will see, before the inspector arrives, is not required by anything. It is the cheapest way to find a display fault before it costs something, and the 8/28 memo was right to suggest it.

How the two audits compare. The driver app had twelve findings, four of them high, including a whole missing record type. The control panel has eight, two of them high, and neither is a missing screen. The pattern is worth noting: the control panel was designed against the memo's list of host reports and follows it closely, while the driver app grew screen by screen and picked up gaps between them.
