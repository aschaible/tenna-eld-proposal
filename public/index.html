Revision 8/28/2026

What the August 17 compliance review changed.

Tenna's FMCSA compliance advisor reviewed the package specifically for host reporting and returned a memo on August 17. "Host," in the regulation's language, is the motor carrier's back-end system and the web surfaces over it, as distinct from the in-vehicle ELD, which is the phone plus the tracker. Most of the memo is about work that belongs on the host and had been drawn on the phone, or had not been drawn at all. Six changes follow from it.

Six changes, in order of consequence

  • New host unidentified-driving report (W16). The memo's central ask, by name: a host-specific report with suggested-reason pop-ups, worked two or three times a week, with 6-month retention. §395.32 makes reviewing and annotating unidentified driving the carrier's duty; the package had no surface for it. This also closes the inventory gap the wireframes flagged for the August 20 session.
  • Unidentified driving shrinks on the phone (D22). The in-vehicle ELD only has to carry the last 8 days of unaccepted records. D22 is now an 8-day claim/reject list with a reason picker (D28) and bulk actions for the high-volume case; it is no longer the place the backlog gets resolved.
  • One transfer family, not both (D17). A device certifies for telematics transfer (web services or email) or peer-to-peer (Bluetooth / USB), never both. The proposal already said as much in its requirement detail; the screen inventory and the wireframe did not. Telematics is elected, and the USB / Bluetooth-to-inspector native module leaves the mobile scope.
  • New host eRODS transfer (W17). FMCSA now requests eRODS transfers for named drivers days before an on-site audit, up to 30 days per driver. W17 does that from the back office and hard-caps each transfer at 30 days, because pushing six months at once fails at FMCSA's end and reads as a failure to produce records.
  • Three fields added to the inspection display (D16), plus a new host inspector view (W18). Sequence number, elapsed engine hours, and accumulated vehicle miles per §4.5.1.1. FMCSA's own example omits them; the advisor's experience is that including them is better received. W18 is the internal-audit rendering of the same thing, shipped from the host.
  • New non-HOS move stamp (D27). The memo lists the four ordinary causes of unidentified driving (mechanic road test, yard movement, yard relocation, OEM warranty movement), and notes that the usual workaround, a shared "exempt driver" account, draws enforcement pushback. D27 lets the named mechanic or yard hand stamp the move in two taps, upstream of the problem.

Effect on the numbers. The screen inventory goes from 41 surfaces to 46: two added on mobile (D27, D28), three on the host (W16, W17, W18). The data element table grows from 37 elements to 47. The 20-week plan and the phase structure are unchanged, and that is a judgement rather than an accident: the three host reports land in Phase 4, and dropping the peer-to-peer transfer build releases roughly the native-module effort they consume. The tradeoff is roughly even, and it should be confirmed in the Phase 2 scope lock rather than assumed. See Section 7.

One item held, not resolved. On the DOT audit export the memo says there seems to be too much available for a DOT audit, and asks for further discussion to confirm the intent. That screen (W13) is unchanged pending that conversation, with the concern recorded against it. The honest possibility is that W13 narrows and W17 carries the real audit workflow.

Every change is marked in place across this package, outlined so it can be found. The control at the bottom right switches between the original and this revision. Screen-by-screen detail is on the Wireframes page ↗; the memo itself and the questions it raises are logged in the Decision Log ↗.

Section 1 · The ELD Mandate

What the regulation requires.

An ELD is a federally-mandated device that automatically records a commercial driver's Hours-of-Service. The rule lives in 49 CFR Part 395, Subpart B, with the device-level technical specification in Appendix A. Self-certification is permitted, but the compliance bar is real. FMCSA revoked 12 devices from the registered list as recently as May 20, 2026.

The 8 categories of ELD self-monitoring

An ELD must continuously monitor itself and emit either a data diagnostic event (a notice, recoverable) or a compliance malfunction (must be remediated within 8 days; driver reverts to paper logs in the meantime). Categories:

Required event records

The ELD records eight categories of events. Each event includes a defined set of data elements (driver ID, vehicle ID, location code with city/state/lat-lon, date/time UTC, engine power status, vehicle motion status, miles driven, engine hours):

  • Duty status changes (OFF, SB, D, ON)
  • Intermediate logs every 60 min while driving
  • Personal Conveyance and Yard Move special duty modifiers
  • Driver certification of the daily log
  • Driver login and logout
  • Engine power on and off
  • Malfunction and diagnostic events (set and clear)
  • Log edits, driver or carrier-proposed, with annotations

The output file

Every ELD must produce a single ASCII ELD output file in a defined format. Header section, user list, CMV list, malfunction and diagnostic log, edit history, event record, unidentified driver log, plus a line-by-line data check (CRC). This file is what gets transferred to a safety official via one of two modes:

  • Telematics ELD type. Wireless web services (HTTPS POST to FMCSA's eRODS endpoint) with email fallback.
  • Local ELD type. USB 2.0 and Bluetooth direct to inspector's device.

An ELD provider chooses one and self-certifies to that mode. Telematics is the modern default and what Motive, Samsara, and Omnitracs all use.

Elected 8/28: Telematics. The August 17 compliance review was pointed about this: you do one family or the other, never both. The original screen inventory drifted into offering all three routes. This build certifies as a Telematics ELD: HTTPS POST to eRODS, email fallback, no USB and no Bluetooth-to-inspector path. It is a certification-time declaration rather than a runtime setting, so it needs Tenna's formal sign-off before Phase 3 begins.

The recent enforcement context

FMCSA revoked 12 ELDs from the Registered Devices List on May 20, 2026 for failing to meet Appendix A, bringing the total recent revocations to roughly 30 devices across the last few rounds. Carriers running revoked devices face an Out-of-Service order at the roadside after the 60-day grace period (July 20, 2026 for the latest batch). The list of registered providers exceeds 700, but it's a long tail. Motive, Samsara, Geotab, and Omnitracs hold the vast majority of vehicles.

Section 4 · Mobile Stack

React Native with strategic native modules.

Three viable approaches: full native (Swift and Kotlin), pure React Native, or React Native with selectively-built native modules. This is a real engineering decision with real timeline implications. The driver application carries three constraints that shape the choice. Reliable Bluetooth pairing with the Tenna tracker. Background processing for HOS tracking when the application is not foregrounded. Roadside inspection display that renders in under 5 seconds without a network. The June 4 session added a fourth: the app ships on both vehicle-mounted tablets and driver personal devices at launch, which widens the device matrix and raises the bar on background reliability. The three options compared below, against what the four incumbent leaders actually run, documented with primary sources in the companion Mobile Stack Evidence Brief ↗.

OPTION A

Full Native (iOS + Android)

Two separate codebases in Swift and Kotlin. Most reliable for Bluetooth, background tasks, and OS integration. Slowest to build and most expensive to maintain.

  • 2 separate codebases
  • Best-in-class Bluetooth and background reliability
  • Platform-idiomatic UX (smoother App Store review)
  • Most expensive to maintain long-term
  • Needs a second mobile engineer or doubles the mobile timeline
  • How Motive builds, sustained by 50-plus mobile engineers
+6 to 10 weeks vs hybrid

OPTION B · RECOMMENDED

React Native + Native Modules

One React Native codebase for UI and business logic. Small native modules in Swift and Kotlin for Bluetooth pairing, background ECM polling, and USB transfer. Best balance of speed and reliability.

One React Native codebase for UI and business logic. Small native modules in Swift and Kotlin for Bluetooth pairing and background ECM polling. Best balance of speed and reliability.

  • About 80% of code shared across platforms
  • Native modules where reliability is non-negotiable
  • 1 mobile engineer ships both platforms
  • The architecture Samsara documents running at scale
  • One codebase to maintain forever
Baseline (current plan)

OPTION C

Pure React Native (or Flutter)

One codebase, no custom native modules. Fastest to build. Risk concentrates in Bluetooth and background processing where cross-platform frameworks have historically been weakest.

  • Fastest initial build
  • Bluetooth reliability concerns under load
  • iOS background task limitations
  • Higher rework risk if reliability issues surface later
-2 to 3 weeks vs hybrid

Why Option B is the recommendation

For most product applications, pure React Native is sufficient. ELDs have a few specific failure modes that justify strategic native code. If Bluetooth pairing drops mid-trip and the application cannot reconnect within 30 minutes, that is an engine sync compliance malfunction under §395.34(a)(2). If iOS aggressively suspends the application and background polling stops, the same outcome. These are not failure modes to discover in production.

The parts that benefit from native code are small and well-bounded. Bluetooth pairing, background ECM polling, and USB transfer collectively represent roughly 10 to 15% of the mobile codebase.The parts that benefit from native code are small and well-bounded. Bluetooth pairing and background ECM polling collectively represent roughly 8 to 12% of the mobile codebase, down from 10 to 15% in the original, because electing the Telematics transfer family in the 8/28 revision removes the USB and Bluetooth-to-inspector module entirely. The remainder, including HOS clock, logs, DVIR, status flows, edit screens, and the roadside display, is standard React Native work that benefits from a single codebase.

Pure native is marginally more reliable on paper. The day-to-day delta is small and the maintenance cost is roughly double. The marginal reliability gain does not justify the timeline and ongoing cost.

What the incumbent leaders actually run

This recommendation is the industry mainstream, not a team-size compromise. Samsara has publicly documented running their Driver App on React Native at scale, including a deliberate rewrite in 2019 that reaffirmed the architectural choice when a Play Store deadline forced the question. Omnitracs is following the same pattern for new builds under Solera ownership. Geotab takes a different approach with a WebView hybrid, where a single web codebase is wrapped in thin native Swift and Kotlin shells. Motive is the outlier, running two fully separate native codebases at the cost of duplicate engineering effort, which is feasible at their team size of more than 50 mobile engineers but not at the scale of this engagement. Three of the four leaders avoid full native because the team-size cost is meaningful. The recommended approach aligns with Samsara's, which is the most directly comparable architecturally.

Section 8 · Certification, Realistically

Tenna self-certifies. AppAxis delivers a device that makes that signature safe.

Unlike FDA approval, FCC certification, or PCI compliance, FMCSA's ELD registration involves no third-party lab, no front-end gatekeeper, and no waiting list. A provider self-certifies on a web form, the device appears on the public registered list within days, and drivers can begin using it. The harder discipline is staying listed when FMCSA audits. In the June 4 session, Tenna took ownership of certification and registration, backed by a dedicated FMCSA compliance expert. AppAxis's job is certification readiness: a device and an evidence trail that make Tenna's self-certification signature defensible.

What submission actually looks like

FMCSA's Equipment Registration portal asks for these fields. There is no fee. This is the part Tenna's FMCSA compliance expert owns.

  • Provider company info (name, address, primary contact)
  • ELD identification (a unique 4-character ELD Identifier, model name, software version)
  • Operating systems list. Each OS variant counts as a separate device bundle (iOS and Android register separately)
  • ELD type. Telematics or Local, which picks the data-transfer mode
  • Self-certification statement, a checkbox affirming the device meets Appendix A
  • URL to the public-facing user instructions. Must be accessible without login
  • Public contact info that appears on the FMCSA registered list

Once submitted, the device typically appears on the public list within a few business days. There is no FMCSA-administered test, no third-party lab certification, and no in-person review. The process operates on the honor system, backed by a credible threat of audit and revocation.

Common revocation triggers

Recent revocations (12 devices in May 2026, on top of earlier rounds totaling approximately 30) trace to a small set of failure modes:

  • Output file format errors. Missing required fields, wrong CRC checksum, wrong section ordering, character encoding mistakes.
  • Malfunction handling gaps. Device fails to detect one of the 8 categories under §395.34(a), or fails to clear correctly when the underlying condition resolves.
  • Engine sync failures. ECM data not polled at the right cadence, or device claims sync when it has lost ECM connection.
  • Edit history gaps. Edits not properly annotated, edit attribution missing, original record not preserved.
  • Unidentified driving not handled. Driving with no driver logged in must be captured and assignable later.
  • Unidentified driving not handled. Driving with no driver logged in must be captured and assignable later. Per §395.32 it must also be reviewed by the carrier and either annotated with a reason or assigned to a driver, with records kept six months. That review needs a host report, which is W16.
  • Driver complaints. Drivers report to FMCSA when an ELD generates impossible logs, drops events, or won't transfer at roadside.

The division of labor

Tenna's FMCSA compliance expert owns the documentation package, the public user guide, the Equipment Registration submission, and the relationship with FMCSA. AppAxis owns certification readiness, which has a concrete exit criterion:

  • The full Appendix A test suite passing on the integrated system. Every event type, every malfunction trigger, every diagnostic threshold, built alongside the code from Phase 3 rather than bolted on at the end.
  • Output files validating clean against FMCSA's reference parser, on every release. Edge cases on edit history, driver-vehicle pairings, and multi-driver scenarios covered.
  • Documented evidence on the malfunction and diagnostic edge cases, the section of the specification that most often results in revocation.
  • Engineering support through Tenna's certification pass during Phase 5, fixing anything the expert's review surfaces.

FMCSA's own review timeline is unpredictable but usually fast. Most providers see their device listed within 1 to 5 business days of submission. The risk window opens later: an audit triggered by a complaint or a random pull, sometimes months out. That later window is exactly what the test suite and the FMCSA compliance expert exist to survive.

After launch: the compliance treadmill

Worth understanding what ongoing compliance involves once the device is listed. With the FMCSA compliance expert in-house, Tenna is well positioned to own this treadmill, with AppAxis available for the code changes it generates.

  • FMCSA spec interpretation updates. The agency issues clarifications and FAQs periodically. Some require code changes.
  • App store re-reviews. Apple and Google occasionally re-review safety-critical apps and ask for changes.
  • Driver-reported edge cases that surface in real production use.
  • Inspector-side issues at roadside transfers. Most are user-facing, a few are real bugs.
  • Annual refresh of the public user guide.
  • Re-submission to FMCSA whenever a major version ships.

Section 10 · Risks and Open Questions

What could derail the plan.

Risks that Phase 2 would surface regardless, presented here for transparency.

Engineering Risks

Tracker Bluetooth broadcast contract

MEDIUM

The tracker already pairs with an in-cab tablet and Tenna's ingestion service is already running, which removes most of the original integration unknown. What remains is the broadcast contract itself: whether the tracker emits all four required ECM elements over Bluetooth at the cadence the specification needs. Validated in Week 1 of Phase 2.

Personal devices as the ELD unit

MEDIUM

Supporting driver personal devices at launch alongside vehicle tablets widens the test matrix: OS fragmentation, aggressive battery managers killing background Bluetooth, and shared-device edge cases. Mitigated by native modules for Bluetooth and background processing plus a defined supported-device list agreed in Phase 2.

Malfunction and diagnostic edge cases

MEDIUM

This is the section of the specification that gets ELDs revoked. Treated as a first-class engineering concern rather than an afterthought. Mitigated by specification-driven testing, though interpretation can vary across implementations.

Unidentified driving volume in short-haul operations

MEDIUM

Raised by the August 17 compliance review: operations where drivers never sign in, short-haul work in particular, can generate excessive unidentified-driving records, and a carrier that does not work the report two or three times a week loses control of it. That is an adoption and support risk more than an engineering one: the software can be correct and the customer still fails an audit. Mitigated on three fronts, all in the 8/28 revision: sticky vehicle pairing so signing in is nearly automatic (D2), a non-HOS move stamp for the people who move trucks without a log (D27), and a host report built for volume with grouping, bulk annotation, and a cadence nudge (W16). The residual question, whether pattern-matched records can be auto-annotated at a carrier level, is open with Tenna's compliance advisor, because blanket treatment is exactly what enforcement pushes back on.

Transfer-family election is a one-way door

LOW

Certifying as a Telematics ELD means no USB or Bluetooth path to an inspector's device, ever, without a re-certification. The 8/28 revision makes that election explicit rather than leaving both families half-drawn. Low risk because it is the mode Motive, Samsara, and Omnitracs all use, but it needs Tenna's formal sign-off before Phase 3 rather than after, and the roadside-with-no-signal case should be walked through with the compliance advisor since email fallback is now the only alternative.

iOS and Android certification cycles

LOW

Apple and Google review for safety-critical applications can take 1 to 2 weeks. Submissions are filed early in Phase 5 to overlap with FMCSA registration.

Specification drift during build

LOW

FMCSA occasionally updates the specification. The last major update was 2024, and the next likely change follows the July 2026 enforcement window. The architecture includes a versioned specification interpretation layer.

Business and Process Risks

FMCSA registration owner and liability

MEDIUM

Tenna becomes the listed provider and therefore inherits liability if a driver receives a violation attributable to an application defect. The planned FMCSA compliance expert materially improves this posture. Legal review and the Errors and Omissions position are still worth confirming.

Certification gate now lives with Tenna

MEDIUM

Tenna owns the step that gates launch. If the FMCSA compliance expert hire slips or the certification pass stalls, the pilot ends with software that is done but not listed. Mitigated by a concrete certification-readiness exit criterion and AppAxis support through the pass in Phase 5.

Existing customer migration

HIGH (IF IN SCOPE)

Moving active customers from the partner ELD to the new platform involves data migration, retraining, and a regulatory cutover. Often as complex as the original build. Recommended approach: handle as a separate phase after the new-sales motion is proven.

Decision approval path inside Tenna

LOW

Knowing which additional stakeholders need to sign off (engineering leadership, finance, legal, customer success) and what concerns they will raise allows the Phase 2 ProposalType to address them directly.

Partner contract exit

MEDIUM

The current partner agreement likely contains a notice period and possibly an exclusivity clause. A legal pre-read is advisable before the new ELD launches publicly.
