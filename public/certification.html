1. Purpose and scope

At the close of Phase 2, Luke asked for a certification-readiness roadmap: a plan that states what has to be decided, what has to be tested, and what evidence has to exist so that Tenna's self-certification of the ELD is defensible and stays defensible under audit. This document is that plan. It is also a named Phase 2 deliverable — the certification-readiness plan that both sides sign before any production code is written.

It extends Section 8 of the proposal, which explains how FMCSA registration works. This document does not repeat that; it lays out the work, stage by stage, with an owner and an exit criterion for each.

2. How ELD certification works, in brief

FMCSA registration is a self-certification. There is no third-party lab, no pre-market approval, and no waiting list. A provider fills in a web form, certifies that the device meets the regulation, and the device appears on the public list within a few business days. There is no fee and no test administered by FMCSA.

The real work is staying listed. FMCSA audits a device after it is registered — often triggered by a driver complaint or a random review, sometimes months after listing. About forty devices have been removed from the list to date, twelve of them in May 2026. The known reasons devices are removed are listed in Section 5, and the stages below are organized around preventing them.

The division of labor was set in the June 4 session and holds throughout. Tenna's FMCSA compliance expert owns the documentation package, the public user guide, the registration submission, and the relationship with FMCSA. AppAxis owns certification readiness: a device, a test suite, and an evidence trail that make the signature defensible. Each stage below states which side owns it.

3. The six stages

Stages A and B run now, inside Phase 2. Stages C through F follow the phase plan in Section 7 of the proposal: build in Phase 3, transfer and host reporting in Phase 4, verification and registration in Phase 5, and ongoing maintenance after the device is listed. A stage is complete when its exit criterion is true and its evidence exists — not before.

Stage A — Hardware validation

Owner: AppAxis · Phase 2, in progress

Everything downstream assumes the tracker's Bluetooth broadcast can carry the four required data elements (engine power, motion, miles, engine hours) reliably — through backgrounding, signal loss, process death, and multi-day operation. The Phase 2 prototype and the bench and drive test protocol prove this on real hardware. Every test run leaves an append-only session log on the device as evidence.

  • The test inventory is Tenna's original 15 connectivity cases (Mark's sheet, unchanged) plus AppAxis-added scenarios 16–31: clock integrity, airplane-mode drives, a 24-hour soak, stored-event drain and purge, process death and gap coverage, the 30-minute §395.34 malfunction escalation, and automatic duty-status transitions on the road.
  • The acceptance criteria for those tests are written against 49 CFR Part 395 and Appendix A, and are still AppAxis proposals, not agreed criteria. Ratifying them is on the advisor agenda (Section 4).
  • Status: the Android re-certification pass currently stands at 25 of 26 protocol steps, with the interrupted stored-event drain the one item left to stage. The iOS pass follows the same protocol.

Exit criterion. Every protocol step passes against advisor-ratified acceptance criteria, on both platforms, and the findings write-up is delivered. Evidence: the completed test workbooks and the per-test session logs pulled from the devices.

Stage B — Compliance requirements freeze

Owner: Joint — the advisors decide, AppAxis drafts, Tenna ratifies · Phase 2 exit

The wireframes now cover all 37 requirements marked MUST, and the 17 August compliance review is folded in. What remains are interpretation questions only the advisors can settle. Each one currently blocks either a data model or a screen from being final. This stage is the scope-lock deliverable the contract names, and it is most of the 3 September session.

  • P3 — raw telemetry retention: what an audit actually requires, and whether device-local retention is enough.
  • Q1 — duty status when the phone loses its link to the vehicle; the deepest open question, and it blocks the Phase 3 HOS engine specification.
  • Q15–Q17 — the unidentified-driving rulings: the non-HOS move stamp's event class, whether rules-based auto-annotation is allowed, and what "unaccepted" and the 6-month retention unit mean.
  • Q18 — the Telematics transfer election, signed. It cannot be reversed later without re-certifying.
  • Q19 and Q20 — the scope of the audit export and whether to build the host inspector view.
  • D29 — the stationary-prompt parameters (window, timeout, recorded origin), confirmed before the production state machine is written.

Exit criterion. The scope lock is signed: every item above has a written advisor answer in the decision log, the HOS engine specification and the data-retention design reflect those answers, and the acceptance criteria are ratified. Evidence: the signed scope-lock package and the updated decision log.

Stage C — Build to Appendix A

Owner: AppAxis · Phase 3, weeks 4–12

The core build: the HOS state machine covering every duty rule and exception, the malfunction and diagnostic engine across all eight categories of §395.34(a), the FMCSA-format output file generator with its line check, and the driver application on real hardware. The QA engineer builds the test harness in the same weeks the code is written, so every Appendix A behavior carries a test from the day it is implemented. That harness is the basis of the readiness dossier in Stage E.

  • Every event type in Appendix A, every malfunction and diagnostic trigger and its clear condition, and every automatic transition (including the D29 stationary prompt) is implemented and tested together.
  • Output files are validated against FMCSA's published file specification continuously, not once at the end.
  • Weekly Friday show-backs so any misreading of the specification is caught in days.

Exit criterion. The Appendix A harness passes for every section implemented, and an output file generated from live test data validates clean. Evidence: the harness run report for each release.

Stage D — Transfer and host reporting

Owner: AppAxis · Phase 4, weeks 13–15

The data-transfer service to eRODS (web services with email fallback, per the Q18 election), with both of its callers: the driver at roadside (D17) and the back office for pre-audit requests (W17, with the 30-day cap per transfer). Plus the host reporting screens the 17 August review asked for: the unidentified-driving report (W16) and, if Q20 lands that way, the host inspector view (W18).

  • Transfer is verified against FMCSA's eRODS test environment before any pilot driver meets an officer.
  • The unidentified-driving loop is closed end to end: tracker capture, the 8-day in-cab list, host review, annotate or assign, driver confirmation (D34), and 6-month retention.
  • Edit and annotation versioning is demonstrated: the original is preserved, attribution is complete, and the trail renders in both the driver and host views.

Exit criterion. An end-to-end system runs on a test fleet, and both a roadside transfer and a host-initiated transfer are accepted by the eRODS test endpoint. Evidence: the transfer confirmations and the test-fleet session record.

Stage E — Certification-readiness verification

Owner: AppAxis assembles, the advisors accept · Phase 5, weeks 16–17

Before Tenna signs anything, the whole case is put in one place and reviewed by the compliance expert as if they were the auditor. This is the exit criterion for AppAxis's side of the engagement.

  • The full Appendix A test suite passing on the integrated system — every event type, every malfunction trigger, every diagnostic threshold — with results archived per release.
  • Output-file validation results, including the edge cases that most often remove devices from the list: edit history, driver-vehicle pairings, and multi-driver scenarios.
  • The malfunction and diagnostic evidence set: each of the eight categories triggered, detected, displayed, and cleared, on record.
  • The on-road test protocol re-run on the customer device matrix Ross provided, on release builds.
  • The in-cab documents §395.22(g)–(h) requires the driver to carry: the user's manual, the data-transfer instruction sheet, the malfunction instruction sheet, and blank paper logs. Drafted by AppAxis, owned and published by Tenna.
  • The public user-instructions page (required by the registration form, and reachable without a login), ready for Tenna's URL.

Exit criterion. Tenna's compliance expert accepts the dossier as sufficient to sign. Evidence: the dossier itself and the expert's written acceptance.

Stage F — Registration, listing, and maintenance

Owner: Tenna, with AppAxis supporting · Phase 5, weeks 17–19, then ongoing

Tenna's compliance expert files the registration: provider information, the four-character ELD identifier, the model name and software version, the operating-systems list (each operating system registers separately, on our current reading — an item to verify with the advisors), the Telematics election from Q18, the self-certification statement, the public instructions URL, and the public contact. Devices typically appear on the list within one to five business days. AppAxis supports the pilot in parallel and fixes anything the certification pass surfaces.

  • After the device is listed, the audit window opens. An audit can arrive by complaint or random review, sometimes months later. The Stage E dossier is what answers it.
  • Ongoing maintenance, owned by Tenna with AppAxis available for the code it requires: FMCSA interpretation updates, re-submission when a major version ships, app-store re-reviews, driver-reported edge cases, and the annual refresh of the user guide.

Exit criterion. The device is listed on the FMCSA registry, and a maintenance runbook — who watches what, who responds to what, and on what schedule — is handed to Tenna and staffed. Evidence: the public listing and the runbook.

4. What this draft needs from the compliance review

Each item below either blocks a stage or shapes one. The full background for the numbered questions is in the decision log; they are gathered here so the review can work through them in order.

P3 — Raw telemetry retention. Blocks Stage B; answer goes to Tamar, then Luke.

Does an FMCSA audit require the raw pre-processing telemetry to substantiate ELD events, or only the distilled event records? If raw data must be kept: which elements, at what granularity, for how long, and is device-local retention acceptable? The answer decides the data pipeline.

Q1 — Duty status when the phone loses the vehicle link. Blocks the Phase 3 HOS engine.

The four sub-questions on record: which component is the ELD of record; whether the tracker's stored stop and start records may be applied retroactively on reconnect; if not, what the correct behavior is; and whether this is a §4.6 malfunction matter rather than a duty-status one.

Q15–Q17 — The unidentified-driving rulings.

The non-HOS move stamp's event class and whether it discharges the review duty (Q15); whether rules-based auto-annotation is allowed at all (Q16); what "unaccepted" means and how the 6-month retention clock is scoped (Q17). These decide the W16, D22, D28, and D34 data model.

Q18 — The Telematics election, formally.

Confirmation that Telematics (web services and email) is the right election for this fleet, a walk-through of the no-coverage roadside case now that the thumb drive is gone, and the election signed before Phase 3. It cannot be reversed without re-certifying.

Q19 and Q20 — Audit export scope, and the host inspector view.

The line-by-line walk-through of the W13 audit export the memo asked for (Q19), and the decision to build or cut the host inspector view, W18, where our recommendation is to build it (Q20).

Acceptance criteria — ratify them.

The bench and drive protocol's acceptance criteria are AppAxis proposals inferred from Part 395 and Appendix A, marked as such on the sheet. Stage A cannot close on self-graded criteria. The advisors ratify, correct, or extend them, including naming any scenario the inventory still lacks.

Stage F — Verify the registration mechanics.

Confirm or correct: whether each operating system registers as a separate device bundle; the constraints on the four-character ELD identifier; the version-string discipline that avoids needless re-submission; what the public instructions URL must contain; and who inside Tenna signs the self-certification statement, and with what diligence behind it.

The roadmap itself — mark it up.

Are six stages the right cut? Are the exit criteria the ones an auditor would recognize? What is missing — state-level programs, Canadian ELD differences if they are in scope, anything the recent removals taught that this document does not reflect?

5. Why these stages — the known reasons devices are removed from the list

The stages are organized around how devices actually lose their registration. Each known reason pairs with the stage and the evidence built to prevent it.

Reason a device is removedHow the roadmap prevents it
Output file format errors — missing fields, wrong line check, wrong ordering, encoding mistakes Stage C validates continuously and Stage E validates per release, so this is never a one-time check.
Malfunction handling gaps — a missed §395.34(a) category, or a condition that never clears Stage C builds the engine with its tests; D32 makes the clear visible; Stage E provides the triggered-and-cleared evidence set.
Engine sync failures — ECM data not read at the right cadence, or sync claimed after it was lost Stage A already tests the cadence watchdog, loss detection, and the 30-minute escalation on hardware.
Edit history gaps — edits unannotated, attribution missing, originals lost Stage C and D versioning preserves the original permanently; Stage E covers the edit-history edge cases in validation.
Unidentified driving not handled — unlogged driving not captured, or captured and never reviewed The tracker's store-and-forward (Stage A), the W16 review loop (Stage D), and the Q15–Q17 rulings (Stage B) settle the model before it is built.
Driver complaints — impossible logs, dropped events, transfers that fail at roadside Stage D verifies transfer against the eRODS test environment; Stage F's pilot puts it in front of real drivers before the public sees it.
